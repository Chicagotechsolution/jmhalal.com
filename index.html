The question

A halal logo is a claim. The certificate behind it is the evidence.

Most of us check for the word "halal" and stop there. This guide is for the step after that one.

Walk any grocery aisle in a big American city and you'll find a dozen halal marks — crescents, Arabic calligraphy, green seals. They look equally official. They are not equally meaningful. Behind each one sits a certifier with its own rules: some require hand slaughter for every animal, some accept machine slaughter for poultry, some audit plants quarterly, some review paperwork once a year from a desk.

JM Halal reads those rules so you don't have to. We explain what each certification mark actually requires, where the standards differ, and which questions remain yours to ask — because no certifier can decide your standard for you.

We're not a certifier, we don't sell certification, and no certifier pays us. We're a guide to the paperwork.

Anatomy of a halal certificate

Five fields tell you most of what you need: who issued it, which facility it covers, which products are in scope, the slaughter method per species, and whether it's still valid.

An expired certificate on the wall is the most common gap we see described by careful shoppers.

The core distinction

Both can appear under a "halal" label. The differences are practical and documented — and whether they matter is a question for your own standard and your own scholar. Our job is to make sure you know which one is in the package.

Reference

A short glossary for reading certifiers' documents.

Zabiha (dhabiha)
Slaughter performed by hand according to Islamic rites: a sane adult Muslim, a sharp blade, the tasmiya recited, and the major vessels of the neck cut swiftly.
Tasmiya
The invocation of God's name pronounced at the moment of slaughter. Certifier policies differ on whether it must be said once per animal.
Stunning
Rendering the animal insensible before slaughter. Some certifiers prohibit it, some permit reversible stunning only, some permit it generally. The certificate or the certifier's published standard will say which.
Scope of certification
The specific products, production lines, or facilities a certificate covers. A certified plant can still run uncertified lines; scope is where that distinction lives.
Audit frequency
How often the certifier physically inspects. Annual desk reviews, scheduled site audits, and unannounced visits are all called "auditing" — they are not the same thing.
Chain of custody
Documentation that halal product stayed segregated from non-halal product through transport, storage, and processing. Breaks in the chain are where mislabeled meat usually enters.
Mashbooh
"Doubtful" — ingredients whose source can't be confirmed, such as unspecified enzymes, gelatin, or flavor carriers. Many certifiers maintain public mashbooh lists.
Self-certification
A business declaring itself halal with no third party involved. Not inherently dishonest — many excellent butchers do it — but it means the evidence is the owner's word, so the questions are yours to ask.

This glossary describes documentation practices, not religious rulings. For what is permissible for you, consult your scholar; for what is in the box, consult the paperwork.
